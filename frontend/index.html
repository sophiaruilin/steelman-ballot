      Methodology

      How we work

      In plain English: where every card comes from, and how we check it before you see it.

      1. We find your ballot

        Your address is matched to your districts by the U.S. Census Bureau. We then read the list of measures from your state's or city's official voter guide. Your address is never stored.

      2. We read only official and nonpartisan sources

        California's Official Voter Information Guide and Legislative Analyst, Massachusetts' official Information for Voters, and New York City's NYC Votes guide. Candidate records come from Congress.gov, Open States and the Federal Election Commission. If sources disagree, the official ballot wins.

      3. An AI writer drafts the card

        It builds a What changes table (today vs. if it passes), then each side's strongest case on the merits: benefits, costs, risks, and who is affected. Never the other side's motives, donors or party. Facts are kept separate from arguments, and every claim is labeled fact, prediction (whose estimate) or value.

      4. A second AI checks it for fairness

        A separate reviewer plays a committed supporter of each side and asks, “Would I sign this as our best case?” It swaps the labels to catch one-sided wording, and checks every claim against the page it came from. Quotes must match word for word. A card gets up to two rewrites; if it still has problems, it's shown with a “steelman unverified” badge.

      5. We measure accuracy

        We graded our California cards against the state's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst: 100% of claims backed by the official guide, 18 of 18 yes/no/cost descriptions correct, and 92% of each side's top points covered.

      6. We keep it fresh and safe

        Cards refresh daily as an election nears. We read only an allowlist of official sites, strip hidden text that could trick an AI, respect sites that ask not to be read automatically, and cap how much the system can spend.

      What we never do

      • Tell you how to vote, or predict a result
      • Show polls or who's “winning”
      • Argue about motives, donors or party labels
      • Store your address, track you, or show ads

      Honest limits

      Coverage is three states for now, and local measures only for New York City. AI can make mistakes: every claim links to its source so you can check, and you can report an issue on any card.

      The idea behind the name

      Steelman vs. strawman

      A strawman is the weakest version of a view, built to be knocked down. A steelman is the strongest version, stated so well that its own supporters would say, “I wish I'd put it that way.”

      Strawman

      “Opponents of the bus lanes just hate buses.”

      Easy to dismiss. Teaches you nothing.

      Steelman

      “Opponents argue that removing car lanes could lengthen trips for people who can't use transit, like night-shift workers and people with disabilities.”

      A real trade-off worth weighing.

      Why it matters for your ballot

      When you only hear the weakest version of the other side, every decision looks obvious. Ballot measures rarely are. Seeing both sides at their strongest lets you test your own view against the best objection, and keep it, change it, or hold it more wisely.

      How we test every steelman

      • Would supporters sign it? A reviewer plays a committed supporter of each side and scores the case from 1 (strawman) to 5 (“I'd sign it”). Below 4 means a rewrite.
      • The swap test. Swap the labels. If one side suddenly sounds warmer, more certain, or more reasonable, the wording gets fixed.
      • On the merits. Arguments must be about what the change would do, not about who supports it or why.
      • Equal weight. Same structure, similar length, same number of points, and no red or blue: neither side is colored as the good guy.

      Based on the principle of charity, Anatol Rapoport's rules for criticism (popularized by Daniel Dennett), and the Ideological Turing Test (Bryan Caplan).

      About us

      Our philosophy

      Democracy works better when people disagree well.

      Most ballots ask voters to settle questions that experts spend months on: taxes, housing, how elections run. The information exists, but it's scattered across official guides, campaign sites and news, and most of what reaches us is written to persuade. Steelman Ballot puts what would actually change next to the strongest case for every side, in plain English, so you can decide for yourself.

      What we believe

      The strongest argument isn't always your own.
      Understanding the best case against your view is how you hold it wisely, or change it.
      All the facts. All the sides. Your decision.
      We inform. We never recommend. Your values are yours.
      On the merits, not the messengers.
      We focus on what a change would do, not on who funds it or why they want it.
      Official sources first, and always cited.
      If anything here disagrees with your official ballot, the official ballot is right.
      Equal treatment, by design.
      Same structure, same space, same tone, same colors for every side and every candidate.
      Your privacy.
      No account, no tracking, no ads. Your address is used once to find your districts and never stored.
